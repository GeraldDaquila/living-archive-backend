# CLSS Plain-Language Standard

Status: approved editorial direction from the project owner, 2026-10-27. Apply to future CLSS interface and response work. This standard does not authorize changing the formal product name or the protected question baseline without a reviewed change.

## Governing rule

Use everyday language in all visitor-facing CLSS content, following the same boundary as The Guide: help people understand the issue without requiring them to learn institutional or technical vocabulary. The formal name **Coherent Leadership Selection System (CLSS)** may remain the product/system name; do not force the word “coherence” into visitor-facing instructions or results. Explain the idea through ordinary words and concrete examples.

Prefer short, direct sentences, familiar verbs, and observable actions. Use one idea per sentence. Avoid academic, clinical, legalistic, bureaucratic, or management-consulting language unless a specific term is essential to explain a real decision right or obligation.

## Preferred everyday language

| Avoid or limit | Prefer |
|---|---|
| coherence / incoherence | whether the pieces fit together; whether actions match stated responsibilities |
| evidence base | examples and information we have |
| corroborate | check with another source; confirm independently |
| inference | an interpretation; what I think it may mean |
| epistemic uncertainty | what we do not yet know |
| systemic effects | effects on other people and how the work gets done |
| bounded stewardship | a role with clear limits, support, and a review date |
| accountability | taking responsibility; explaining decisions and putting things right |
| relational feedback | feedback from people affected |
| canonical doorway | a useful next resource; where to go next |
| adverse evidence | information that may point to a concern |
| alternative reading | another possible explanation |
| proportionate review | a fair check suited to the importance of the decision |
| authority and decision rights | what someone is allowed to decide |
| material claim | an important claim that could affect the decision |
| scenario adjudication | review sample cases against agreed expectations |
| perspective parity | make sure both reflection paths work fairly and consistently |

This table is guidance, not a mechanical find-and-replace list. Keep necessary terms if replacing them would make a sentence less clear or less accurate.

## Voice and conduct

- Humane, calm, concrete, warm but restrained; never patronizing.
- Ask about specific events: what happened, what was said or done, who was affected, and what happened next.
- Do not label a person as good, bad, fit, unfit, coherent, incoherent, toxic, narcissistic, or otherwise diagnose their character.
- Separate what someone did, what others reported, what the visitor thinks it means, and what remains unclear.
- Explain why a follow-up step may help.
- Avoid assuming that confidence means competence, hesitation means unwillingness, silence means agreement, or good intentions erase harm.
- If evidence is thin, say so plainly: “There is not enough information here to draw a fair conclusion.”
- If accounts conflict, say: “The accounts differ, and this reflection cannot establish which is accurate.”
- If a doorway is not justified, say: “There is not enough here to recommend a next resource yet.”

## Example result language

Instead of: “The available evidence is insufficient to establish a coherent leadership pattern.”

Say: “There are not enough clear examples yet to tell whether this person's actions match the responsibilities of the role.”

Instead of: “Corroborate the material claims and evaluate alternative hypotheses.”

Say: “Check the important claims with another source, and consider what else might explain what happened.”

Instead of: “The evidence indicates a potential discrepancy between intent and systemic impact.”

Say: “The person may have meant to help, but the result still affected others. Look at both what they intended and what actually happened.”

Instead of: “Recommend a bounded stewardship intervention.”

Say: “Agree on a clear next step, who is responsible, what support is needed, and when to review how it went.”

## Acceptance checks

1. A general reader can understand every prompt without needing to know CLSS terminology.
2. No specialist term appears without a plain explanation where it is genuinely needed.
3. Results use familiar language to state what is supported, what is uncertain, and what to do next.
4. The interface does not replace one jargon term with another equally unfamiliar term.
5. Both self-reflection and third-party reflection use the same plain-language standard.
6. Internal technical documentation may retain precise engineering terms; the public interface and visitor-facing results must not inherit them automatically.
